The Last Territory

How highways, development, and habitat loss reshaped the Florida panther’s survival.

A Species Reduced to Space

Once roaming across the southeastern United States, the Florida panther’s historic range stretched from Texas to the Carolinas. By the mid-20th century, unregulated hunting, agricultural clearing, and rapid urban development reduced the population to fewer than 30 individuals.

The collapse was not merely biological — it was geographic. Highways fragmented habitat. Subdivisions replaced wetlands. The panther’s territory shrank southward into isolated pockets of Southwest Florida.

Highways as Barriers

As suburban development expanded north of Naples and Fort Myers, road networks increasingly cut across panther habitat. Vehicle collisions have become the leading documented cause of panther mortality in recent decades.

Wildlife underpasses along I-75 have reduced deaths, but connectivity remains fragile. Without expanded corridors, population growth stalls at geographic limits.

From Collapse to Fragile Recovery

In 1995, eight female Texas cougars were introduced to prevent genetic collapse. The intervention succeeded. Population estimates climbed steadily. Yet recovery remains geographically constrained.
